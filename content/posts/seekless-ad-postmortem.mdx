[Seekless](https://www.seekless.ai) is a job search tool I have been building this year. It reads company career pages directly, lets you search them in plain language, and has a few tools on top: a free résumé checker, résumé tailoring for a specific job, and interview practice.

In October I ran a paid video campaign to get people to remember it. Over four weeks I spent $614.75 on short video ads across YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Instagram and Reddit. Those ads were shown 221,642 times and clicked 1,322 times. They produced 3 engaged visits and 0 sign-ups.

I want to write down what happened, because most of what I read about startup marketing describes the campaigns that worked. This one did not, and the reasons are more useful than the result.

## The bet

This was not my first paid campaign. Earlier Reddit ads (in July and October) had spent $636.92, and ChatGPT ads (from September 26 to October 10) had spent $427.52. Both were set up to buy clicks, and neither produced a sign-up I could see. That evidence was weak, because sign-ups were not being recorded for much of that time. But the pattern was clear enough: weekly visitors rose from about 40 to 60 to about 230 while the ads ran, and fell straight back when they stopped.

So I changed the goal. Someone scrolling a feed is not looking for a job at that moment. Their intent is close to zero. Instead of trying to make them click, I wanted Seekless to stick with them, so that they would come back on their own later, when they were actually searching. The feed ads were set up to buy reach and views, not clicks.

The success measure followed from that. I was getting 3 to 4 sign-ups a week that did not come from an ad, and the target was 8 a week by the end of the four weeks.

The plan:

- $1,500 over four weeks, with an early read at week two
- $60 a day across four platforms: TikTok $20, YouTube Shorts $15, Instagram $15 and Reddit $10
- two videos of about 22 seconds each, run everywhere, so the campaign would also test which opening worked better

Video A opened with a pile: job boards, résumé files and a tracking spreadsheet stacking up, then "one place for the whole search". Video B opened with a question: "Still sending the same résumé to every job?" I liked B best from the start.

## What happened

The campaign went live on the evening of October 19.

B beat A on every downstream measure where the two were compared. On YouTube, B drew 334 clicks in the first week to A's 133. Meta put most of the Instagram budget behind B on its own. TikTok bought completed views more cheaply than anything else, at about $0.04 each.

None of that turned into use. After the first week the four platforms had spent $416.82 for 163,758 impressions, and the result was 2 engaged visits and 0 sign-ups. That is about $208 for each visit where someone actually did something on the site.

Meanwhile I had started a small Google Search campaign on October 22, aimed at people searching for things like a résumé checker. From October 22 to 26 it spent $89.56 and produced 27 finished résumé checks, about $3.32 each.

On October 26 I stopped TikTok, Instagram, Reddit and ChatGPT. I kept YouTube running with video B only, moved the main focus to Search, and made two more YouTube videos built on what B did well: C pointed at the résumé checker, and D at résumé tailoring.

They did not help. From October 26 to November 2, YouTube spent $199.75 and brought 506 sessions from its ads. One of those became an engaged visit. None became a sign-up. Video D hit a rule I had agreed to in advance, $40 of spend with no outcome, and was paused. Then I looked at YouTube as a whole: about $200 per engaged visit, against about $5.06 per finished résumé check on Search from October 27 to November 2. I stopped YouTube that evening and moved its $25 a day to Search, two weeks before the rule for ending it was due.

The final numbers for the video campaign:

- $614.75 spent of the $1,500 budget
- 221,642 impressions and 1,322 clicks
- YouTube bought the cheapest clicks, at about $0.26 each
- 3 engaged visits, 0 finished résumé checks and 0 sign-ups from people who arrived through the ads

## The measure I could not read

The success measure, sign-ups that did not come from an ad, did not move while the ads ran. It was 2 a week in both live weeks, against 3 to 6 a week before launch.

It also became unreadable. A scraper started hammering the site during the campaign. Sessions that did not come from a campaign went from 143 in the week of October 12 to 1,518 the next week and 12,120 the week after that. Most of that was one automated browser with old user agents, so the before-and-after comparison the plan depended on could not be made.

Sign-ups did jump later: 44 in the week of November 2 and 198 the week after. That came after every feed ad had stopped. Most of those accounts carried a click id from an ad, and the rise lines up with other things: Search ads for interview practice, posting the same videos as regular unpaid posts on November 2, and a separate practice campaign. Many of those sign-ups were also pranks (more on that below). None of it is credited to the video campaign.

## Intent beats attention, mostly

The clearest lesson is the contrast with Search. People who search for a résumé checker want a résumé checker. About 43% of the sessions from the résumé checker ads ended with a finished check.

Search was not a clean win either, though. A finished check is not a sign-up. Only 2 of the people who finished a check from those ads made an account. Counting all of the first Search campaign's spend from October 26 to November 12, including the ad groups that did not work, a finished check cost about $7.57.

And intent can mean the wrong thing. Search ads for interview practice drew a wave of sign-ups on phones. On November 11 and 12 there were 34 practice calls, and 22 of them were people pranking the AI interviewer. Getting people to do something is not the same as getting the right people to do the right thing.

## Why the video campaign failed

Looking back, the campaign never really tested the idea it was built on.

- **Too thin to build memory.** $60 a day spread across four platforms meant each person saw an ad about once. An awareness campaign usually needs three or more views per person before anything sticks. I bought single views on four platforms instead of repeated views on one.
- **The ad and the page said different things.** On October 23, a few days in, I changed the home page to lead with "Tailor your résumé. Practice the interview." The ads still said "one place for the whole job search". Someone who clicked landed on a page making a different promise.
- **I could not see the result.** The measure depended on counting unpaid sign-ups in the analytics tool, and a scraper flooded exactly that count.

## How it was run

One part of this worked well, and it is the part I would keep.

The whole campaign was run by an AI agent working through the ad platforms' APIs. It built every campaign paused, dry-ran each change before making it, and read the state back after every change to confirm it took. It kept a dated record of every decision with my reasoning attached, and a check-in log with the figures and where each one came from.

The rules for stopping things were written down before launch. The agent proposed stops either from those rules, like video D's $40 rule, or from the latest figures, like the feed platforms and YouTube. I made every call. Because the rules and numbers were already on the table, those calls took a few minutes instead of a weekend of second-guessing, and I stopped spending money on things that were not working early.

It also ran into the usual friction. The Google Ads API cannot create the YouTube campaign type I first wanted, so the agent used another type that the API does support. Meta would not take a card until the ad account had a country, and that had to be set through the API. ChatGPT ads would not accept a daily budget under $25.

What the agent could not do was fix the bet itself. It ran a weak plan carefully. A careful process makes it cheap to find out you are wrong, but it does not make you right.

## What I would do next time

- **Pick one platform and buy repeated views.** Three or more views per person on one feed, instead of one view each on four.
- **Land on a page that says what the ad says.** If the message changes, the ads change with it.
- **Measure from the product, not the analytics tool.** Attribution and product activity in my own database are much harder for a scraper to distort than unpaid visitor counts.
- **Read the Search campaigns before crediting the feeds.** When several things change at once, the boring explanation is usually the right one.

For now the money goes to Search, on the free résumé checker. It is less exciting than a brand video, but it is where people are already looking.
