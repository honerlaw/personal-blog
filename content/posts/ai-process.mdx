AI in the age of a platform team.

Around a year ago, I moved over to lead the Backend Frameworks team at Credit Karma. This team focuses on building 3 critical pieces of technology. A write once, multi-runtime SDK (named Polly), and two frameworks integrating the SDK targetted towards Node and the JVM (named Talon). The Talon frameworks are legacy frameworks that have been around for 8 years, and Polly is a relatively new initiative that began around three years ago. In my first 9 months on the team, I refocused us to modernizing our aging frameworks, and on finalizing Polly as the backbone of those frameworks. Overall, this has been successful, but the main pain point that most platform teams run into, is how do we engage and accelerate adoption of the latest variations of these frameworks.

At Credit Karma, all (well mostly all) services are built on top of the Talon framework. This means that in order to upgrade, they need to update their dependencies and redeploy their service. Coordinating this effort everytime we have an impactful release across hundreds of services has been a huge pain point for the last several years and one that I was brought on to help solve.

In the age of AI, everyone seems to latch on to that as the only mechanism or the primary mechanism to drive this type of adoption. However, we took a multi-prong approach in order to improve overall adoption rates, while decreasing the risk that service owners take on when upgrading such a critical dependency to their service.

So how did we drive adoption, and how are we driving adoption into the future in a way that has minimal impact on teams? There was a clear set of areas that we had to address in order to make this happen. This was both a problem externally and internally within the team.

Interal Friction

- Fear to make changes that could break service owners
- High support costs with every framework upgrade
- Very low confidence in the stability of a given framework release
- Every feature touched multiple languages, polygots were a requirement

External Friction

- Upgrades were seen as extremely time consuming (weeks to months for some service owners)
- Upgrades were considered extremely risky
- Broken trust in what types of changes were released in the framework
- Broken trust in release velocity of the frameworks
